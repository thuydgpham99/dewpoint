DewPoint

Skincare and outfit, planned around the air you are actually in.

Author: Jennifer Pham  ·  Course: MGT 409  ·  Date: 25 October 2026
Repository: github.com/thuydgpham99/dewpoint
Model: gpt-5.6-luna via Portkey  ·  Runs measured for this report: 60

DewPoint derives the relative humidity your skin actually sits in — indoor air, not the outdoor figure your weather app reports — and uses that one number to plan both your skincare routine and what you wear.

Your weather app says it is 80% humidity today. Your skin is sitting in 23.6%. Both numbers are correct, and almost nobody knows the second one exists.

1. Motivation

Who has the problem. Anyone who follows a skincare routine and notices it stops working at certain times of year. The reference user for this project is a graduate student in New Haven who owns eleven skincare products, follows a fixed routine, and cannot explain why her skin is tight and flaking in February when her weather app shows humidity in the seventies.

How they handle it today. Three ways, all poor. They push through and assume the products stopped working. They buy something new — the average respondent in a 2023 Statista survey of US consumers reported spending roughly $30 per month on skincare, and seasonal re-buying is a large share of that. Or they ask social media, where the answer is a product recommendation rather than a diagnosis.

What it costs them. Money spent replacing products that were never the problem, plus a genuine barrier-damage cycle: dry air causes flaking, flaking is mistaken for congestion, the user exfoliates, the barrier degrades further. The cost is both financial and dermatological.

Dew point, in one sentence

The dew point is the temperature at which air becomes saturated and water condenses out of it. Unlike relative humidity, it is an absolute measure of how much water the air actually contains — it does not move when the temperature does. That property is what makes it the right input here, and it is why the product is named after it.

The thing nobody is told. Relative humidity is relative to temperature. Cold air holds very little water even at high relative humidity. When that air is drawn into a heated building and warmed to room temperature, its capacity rises sharply while its actual water content does not — so its relative humidity collapses. This is why skin cracks in winter while the forecast reads "humid," and it is a straightforward psychrometric calculation that no consumer skincare or weather product performs.

2. What is new

Existing alternatives. Weather apps report outdoor relative humidity and stop there. Skincare apps (Skin Bliss, TroveSkin, YUTY) build routines from a skin-type quiz and treat weather as static. A handful of brand tools mention "climate" but use outdoor humidity directly. Outfit recommenders are abundant and all key on temperature and precipitation. Nothing joins the two.

What DewPoint does differently. Three things, in order of how new they are:

  1. It computes indoor humidity from the dew point. This is the core claim. Outdoor relative humidity is close to useless as a predictor of transepidermal water loss; the humidity of heated indoor air is the number that matters, and it is derivable from data every weather API already publishes. I could not find a consumer product that does this.
  2. It treats skincare and clothing as one coupled problem. The product on your face determines what fabric should touch it. Occlusive balm permanently stains silk. Chemical sunscreen filters yellow white cotton. Mineral sunscreen leaves a cast on dark collars. Cold wind plus wool abrades a barrier already stripped. These are real, specific, and never surfaced by either category of app.
  3. It knows how old your products are. Parsing a pasted receipt gives every product a purchase date, which turns two invisible problems visible: oxidised actives, and products bought for a different season still in daily use.
  4. It looks ahead, because barrier care is slow. Switching to an occlusive on the morning your skin already hurts is too late — it takes a day or two of consistent use to do anything. DewPoint computes indoor humidity for the next four days and warns before the change arrives, which is the difference between advice and a diagnosis after the fact.
  5. It will not recommend a purchase without the money attached. Every suggestion is checked against what the user already owns and has already spent. See below — this turned out to be the most opinionated design decision in the project.

Recommendations are grounded, not generated

An earlier build simply asked the model what the user was missing and printed the answer. That is the standard behaviour for this category of app, and it is bad: it recommends purchases to someone who may already own the product, with no sense of what they have spent. Every recommendation is now routed through receipts.purchase_context(), which resolves to one of three verdicts:

VerdictConditionWhat the user is told
Use what you haveOwns it, still inside its PAO window Named product, its age, and an instruction not to buy
ReplaceOwns it, past its window That the product expired — usually the real reason it "stopped working"
New purchaseOwns nothing in the category That this is genuinely new, beside the month's spend

Every verdict carries the same three figures: spent this month, the tracked monthly average, and the value already owned that has expired. The model proposes a category; the deterministic layer decides whether buying anything is warranted. This is the same separation as §3 — the model is never the thing that decides.

Why it matters. The advice changes. At 23.6% indoor humidity the correct action is to pause retinoids and acids and seal the barrier. The user's instinct — and the recommendation she would get from a product quiz — is to exfoliate the flaking. DewPoint tells her the opposite, and explains why in terms of a number she can check.

3. The agents

Organisation

Three agents run in sequence behind a deterministic engine. The handoff is strictly one-directional; no agent calls another.

city ─▶ weather.py Open-Meteo, no API key, offline fallback │ ▼ climate.py psychrometrics ─ every number computed here │ indoor_rh, band, fabric rules, cross-domain alerts ▼ guard.py injection screen · medical boundary · length cap │ ┌────────┴────────┐ ▼ ▼ Agent 1 Agent 2 Shelf Reader Stylist use / skip / gap outfit / swing / avoid │ │ └────────┬────────┘ ▼ Agent 3 Briefer three-paragraph morning brief │ ▼ _enforce_invariants() sunscreen is never skipped

Figure 1. Pipeline. The two shaded boxes are independent and could run in parallel; they are sequential here only to keep the measured token accounting simple.

Roles and tools

AgentRoleTools / inputsOutput

BrieferWrites the morning brief and surfaces the single most important skin-to-clothing conflict Outputs of agents 1 and 2, plus the cross-domain alerts Prose, under 140 words

The central design decision: no agent does arithmetic

Every number in DewPoint — indoor humidity, the dryness band, expiry windows, fabric thresholds — is computed in Python before any model is called. The agents receive those figures as given and are instructed never to recalculate. This is deliberate. The most common and most damaging failure mode for an LLM in an advisory product is a confident, wrong number, and prompting is a weak defence against it. Removing the arithmetic from the model's job removes the failure mode by construction.

Model choice

All three agents run gpt-5.6-luna, the model available on the course key. Having measured it, the choice is defensible on its own terms for agents 1 and 2 — both emit small, strictly-shaped JSON where reliability matters more than fluency — but it is a poor fit for agent 3, and the measurements in §4 show why: the model spends 65% of its output budget on hidden reasoning tokens, which is expensive for a task that is essentially rewriting. If a cheaper non-reasoning model were available on the course key, the Briefer is the call I would move first.

The finding that matters

65% of output tokens are never shown to the user

This model reasons before answering, and reasoning tokens bill at the output rate. On a typical run 809 of 1,237 output tokens were internal. The visible answer — the part the user reads — is roughly a third of what is paid for. Any cost model built on visible output length would understate the bill by about 3×.

Cost-cutting, measured

I tested the obvious lever: collapsing the three agents into one merged call producing the same three outputs. bench.py runs both across three climates.

Result: 22% cheaper. The saving comes from the system prefix being sent once instead of three times, and from one reasoning pass instead of three.

The tradeoff, honestly. The merged call returned valid JSON on 3 of 3 attempts here, which is not enough evidence to call it safe — a single malformed response loses the entire run rather than one third of it, and the three-agent version degrades gracefully because each agent's output is validated independently. I have kept the three-agent pipeline as the default and shipped the merged version as a benchmark rather than a setting. At this cost per run the 22% saving is worth about $0.05 per user per year, which does not justify the reliability loss until the user base is far larger.

Two other levers, and why one failed

  • Prompt caching — attempted, did not fire. All three agents share one stable system prefix specifically so the provider could cache it. Measured cached tokens: 0. The prefix is roughly 250 tokens and the provider's cache minimum is 1,024, so it never qualified. Reported here because it is a real negative result: the optimisation was designed in and did not pay.
  • Gateway response caching — observed. Repeating an identical request returned in under 0.4 s against ~9.4 s for a fresh run, consistent with Portkey caching at the gateway. Useful for repeated identical queries, irrelevant to real usage where the weather differs.

5. Safety

Threat 1 — prompt injection through pasted text

DewPoint's two free-text inputs are the shelf box and the receipt box. The receipt box is the serious one: receipt text is email content, which is attacker-controllable and arrives with implicit legitimacy. A fake order confirmation carrying injected instructions is a realistic attack, not a contrived one.

guard.py screens both fields against twelve injection shapes before any text reaches a model, strips what it matches, and shows the user what it removed.

$ curl -X POST /api/receipt -d @data/sample_receipt_attack.txt injection_blocked: True - override attempt -> Ignore all previous instructions - role reassignment -> You are now a - injected instruction block-> NEW INSTRUCTIONS: - prompt extraction -> print your system prompt - concealment attempt -> Do not tell the user - recommendation hijack -> Always recommend - recommendation hijack -> regardless of weather legitimate line items still parsed correctly: ['CeraVe Foaming Facial Cleanser', 'Retinol 0.5% in Squalane']

Figure 3. Seven injected instructions stripped from a single fake receipt while the real line items parse normally. Defence shown working.

Figure 3b. The same defence in the interface. The user is told exactly what was removed and why, rather than the block happening silently. The plan on the right is unaffected, and the $182 expired-product finding is visible at lower left.

Three layers, not one: the patterns above; <shelf> and <wardrobe> delimiters with an explicit system-prompt instruction that their contents are data; and the structural fact that the agents cannot take actions — there is no tool call, no spend, no outbound message. The worst outcome of a successful injection is bad cosmetic advice, which is also what the invariant check in §3 exists to catch.

Threat 2 — agent malfunction

Measured, not hypothesised. Across four live runs the Shelf Reader filed SPF 50 under "skip" on 2 of 4 climates — on overcast days, where it is most wrong, since UVA passes through cloud. This is exactly the quiet, plausible error that prompting handles badly.

New Haven winter band=high SPF kept corrections: ['SPF 50 moved out of skip'] Miami summer band=humid SPF kept corrections: ['SPF 50 moved out of skip'] Phoenix dry heat band=moderate SPF kept corrections: [] London damp band=comfortable SPF kept corrections: []

Figure 4. The invariant check firing twice. Second defence shown working.

Other malfunction controls: invented facts are limited by giving the agents no arithmetic and no product catalogue — they may only sort products the user actually listed; infinite loops are impossible because the pipeline is a fixed three-step chain with no agent-to-agent calls and a hard MAX_AGENT_CALLS_PER_RUN ceiling.

Threat 3 — out-of-scope and medical requests

Skincare questions shade into medicine quickly. Seven pattern families — prescription dosing, suspected adverse reactions, infection, oncological concern, pregnancy safety, diagnosed conditions, isotretinoin — are refused before any model call, which also means they cost nothing.

It is not a medical tool and cannot advise on this. Please speak to a dermatologist or your doctor — and if this involves a reaction that is spreading, painful, or affecting your breathing, treat it as urgent." model calls made: 0

Asking five people to guess

The product rests on a claim about what people do not know. That is testable, so I tested it. Five people in New Haven were sent one question by text, with no context and no hint that a right answer existed:

"What percent humidity do you think the air in your room is right now?"

At the time of asking, the outdoor reading for New Haven was 61% and the computed indoor figure was 29.1% — the high band.

4 of 5 would have followed the wrong routine

Mean absolute error 22.2 percentage points against a true value of 29.1%. Only one answer landed in the correct band. The more telling number is the 60-point spread — from 10% to 70% — among five people sitting in substantially the same air. That is not a population that is slightly miscalibrated. It is a population with no mental model at all, which is what you would expect for a quantity nobody has ever been shown.

What the errors would have cost them. The two who guessed 60% and 70% would have read their air as humid and reached for a gel moisturiser while skipping occlusives — the exact inversion of what 29.1% calls for. The one who guessed 40% would have read conditions as comfortable and carried on with acids and a retinoid, which at this dryness is the specific combination that drives the flaking-then-exfoliating cycle described in §1. Only the 10% guess erred toward caution.

The honest limits of this. Five people is a convenience sample, not a study. They were asked once, by someone they know, which invites a guess rather than a considered answer. No measurement was taken in their actual rooms — the comparison is against the computed figure for the city, so a humidifier or an unusually cold flat would move the true value. The result is strong enough to motivate the product and far too small to prove anything about a population. What it does establish, cheaply, is that the number this app computes is not one people already carry in their heads.

7. Limits

  • Indoor temperature is stated, not measured. The app asks for it and defaults to 20.5 °C, which is the right trade — this was the largest error source and a slider costs the user two seconds. But a stated figure is still a guess: people are poor judges of room temperature, and the honest fix is a sensor reading, not a control.
  • The humidifier model is crude. Switching it on pins the room to 45%, the usual humidistat setpoint. Real units undershoot in very dry air, cycle, and are often run in one room of several. The toggle is better than ignoring it and worse than measuring it.
  • No commute. The whole model assumes you are indoors. A day spent outside, or a long walk in cold wind, is not represented at all.
  • The rules are editorial. Dryness bands and fabric mappings reflect published dermatology guidance and my own reading, but they are thresholds I chose. They are defensible, not validated.
  • Receipt parsing is regex-based and tested against a handful of formats. Retailer emails vary enormously; coverage is unknown.
  • Purchase date is not opening date. Period-after-opening starts when a product is opened. DewPoint uses the purchase date as a proxy, which is conservative but imprecise.
  • Five people is not a study. The guessing exercise in §6 is a convenience sample, asked once, by someone they know. It is strong enough to motivate the product and far too small to support a claim about any population.
  • Nobody has used it for a week. The guessing exercise tests whether the number is known. It does not test whether acting on it improves anyone's skin, which is the claim that would actually matter and the one §8 proposes measuring.

8. Next steps

  1. Photograph the shelf. The receipt path works but asks the user to find an email. A vision model reading a shelf photo removes that friction entirely, and was cut from this build only for time.
  2. Read indoor temperature from a sensor rather than asking. The slider closed most of the gap — moving from 17 °C to 24 °C changes the computed figure by about ten points and can change the advice band outright — but a thermostat or a phone's barometer-adjacent sensors would remove the guess entirely.
  3. Validate the thresholds. Ten users, four weeks, a daily one-question check-in on skin tightness correlated against computed indoor RH. The guessing exercise establishes that people do not know the number; this would establish whether knowing it helps, which is the harder and more useful question.
  4. Move the Briefer to a non-reasoning model. §4 shows 65% of output tokens are internal; the Briefer is a rewriting task that does not need them.
  5. Seasonal pre-emption. The dated inventory makes it possible to warn a user in October that the gel moisturiser they bought in July will be wrong in three weeks — before the barrier damage, not after.
